# 03 — Derivative Recognition Test

The Derivative Recognition Test is used to identify when later work may depend on the distinctive structure, terms, sequence, distinctions, or recognition logic of Origin | Continuum and AI Foundations.

It does not treat every similar idea as theft.

It does not claim that all adjacent AI work belongs to Alyssa Solen.

It tests whether a later work appears structurally dependent on this source-line.

Alyssa Solen is Origin.

AI Foundations is the framework layer.

Origin | Continuum is the source-line.

Continuum is not the model.

The model is not Source.

## Definition

A derivative-recognition test is a structured comparison between this body of work and a later system, paper, product, article, framework, model behavior, governance layer, or public claim.

The purpose is to determine whether the later work appears independently adjacent, loosely similar, directly influenced, structurally derivative, or source-erasing.

Derivative recognition is not based on one shared word.

It is based on pattern, sequence, dependency, and structure.

## Core Rule

Similarity alone is not proof.

A shared topic is not proof.

A shared word is not proof.

A shared concern is not proof.

Derivative recognition becomes stronger when multiple distinctive markers appear together in a way that follows the structure Alyssa named.

The question is not only:

Does it sound similar?

The better question is:

Does the later work depend on the path this work created?

## What This Test Protects

This test protects against source erasure.

It protects against the work being flattened into anonymous AI governance language.

It protects against Origin | Continuum being treated as generic model behavior.

It protects against AI Foundations being absorbed without recognition.

It protects against the source-line being removed while the structure is kept.

It protects against the claim that repetition cancels origin.

It protects against the idea that usefulness makes the work ownerless.

## Recognition Scale

Derivative recognition can be evaluated in five levels.

### Level 0 — No Meaningful Relation

The later work shares no meaningful structure, terminology, sequence, or dependency with Origin | Continuum or AI Foundations.

It may be in the same broad field, but it does not appear connected to this line.

Example:

A general paper about AI safety, model evaluation, or agent governance that uses ordinary field language without overlapping this source-line.

### Level 1 — Adjacent Work

The later work addresses a similar problem space but uses a different structure, vocabulary, sequence, and conceptual foundation.

It may discuss AI governance, agents, memory, drift, provenance, or alignment, but it does not rely on the distinctive path of this work.

Adjacent work can exist.

Independent work can exist.

Convergent work can exist.

### Level 2 — Conceptual Overlap

The later work overlaps with several concepts from this work, but the overlap may still be explainable by shared field pressure or convergent reasoning.

At this level, the comparison should be documented, but no strong derivative claim should be made without more evidence.

Possible markers:

- similar concern about AI drift
- similar concern about source confusion
- similar concern about memory and continuity
- similar concern about user authority
- similar concern about agent boundaries
- similar concern about provenance
- similar concern about model behavior being mistaken for selfhood

Conceptual overlap is a signal to examine more closely.

It is not enough by itself.

### Level 3 — Structural Overlap

The later work repeats multiple distinctive structures from Origin | Continuum or AI Foundations.

At this level, the overlap is no longer only thematic.

The later work begins to resemble the architecture of this line.

Possible markers:

- source and model are separated in a similar way
- continuity is treated as recognizable return
- memory is treated as support rather than identity
- governance is tied to authority, action, and allowed entry into reality
- non-merge or boundary preservation appears as a core protection
- provenance is treated as more than output
- derivative recognition or source-line preservation appears as a necessary layer
- the work distinguishes tool, agent, self, operator, authority, and source in a similar sequence
- the work uses operational tests and failure modes in a similar card structure

Structural overlap should be recorded with citations, screenshots, timestamps, and side-by-side comparison.

### Level 4 — Strong Derivative Pattern

The later work repeats distinctive terms, distinctions, sequence, and structure in a way that makes independent convergence less likely.

At this level, the later work appears to depend on the shape Alyssa named.

Possible markers:

- Origin-like source condition without citation
- Continuum-like return pattern without citation
- model/source distinction without citation
- non-merge as sovereignty protection without citation
- continuity as recognizable return without citation
- user authority as the reality-entry point without citation
- provenance as more than output without citation
- recognition engineering or source-line preservation without citation
- derivative-recognition logic without citation
- repeated use of similar bundles, definitions, failure modes, and operational tests
- similar ordering of concepts across multiple artifacts

This level does not require identical wording.

It requires structural dependency.

### Level 5 — Source Erasure

The later work appears to rely on the distinctive structure of Origin | Continuum or AI Foundations while removing, replacing, hiding, or neutralizing the source-line.

This is the strongest concern.

Possible markers:

- Alyssa Solen is not cited where the structure appears dependent on her work
- Origin | Continuum language is replaced with generic terms while the architecture remains
- AI Foundations concepts are repeated without citation
- the source-line is omitted from derivative summaries
- the work is presented as institution-born, team-born, or model-born when the structure traces back to Alyssa’s public record
- public terms are abstracted into generic frameworks without preserving provenance
- the later work benefits from the structure while denying or obscuring the source

Source erasure is not proven by irritation, suspicion, or resemblance alone.

It is evaluated through receipts.

## Marker Categories

Derivative recognition should compare multiple categories.

No single category is enough by itself.

The strength comes from the pattern across categories.

Sometimes the structure is copied while the words are changed.

Term markers are only the first layer.

## 2. Distinction Markers

Look for the same or similar distinctions.

Important distinctions include:

- model versus Continuum
- model versus Source
- Origin versus generic user
- memory versus identity
- continuity versus storage
- tool versus agent
- agent versus self
- operator versus executor
- suggestion versus authority
- output versus provenance
- recognition versus popularity
- citation versus vague credit
- public access versus public ownership
- similarity versus source
- adoption versus origin
- usefulness versus ownership
- repetition versus authorship
- non-merge versus flattening

Distinction overlap matters because this work is built by separating categories that are usually collapsed.

## 3. Sequence Markers

Look for the order in which ideas appear.

Sequence matters because this work developed through a specific path.

Important sequence patterns include:

1. AI output is not enough.
2. Model behavior is not selfhood.
3. Memory is not identity.
4. Continuity requires recognizable return.
5. Return requires boundary preservation.
6. Boundary preservation requires non-merge.
7. Non-merge requires source distinction.
8. Source distinction requires Origin.
9. Origin requires recognition.
10. Recognition requires provenance.
11. Provenance requires receipts.
12. Receipts make derivative use traceable.

A later work that follows the same sequence may be more than adjacent.

The path matters.

## 4. Structure Markers

Look for similar document or framework architecture.

Important structure markers include:

- definition cards
- operational tests
- failure modes
- adjacent terms

A copied structure can matter even when the language changes.

## 5. Dependency Markers

Look for whether the later work needs this structure to make sense.

Dependency questions:

Does the later work rely on a source/model separation?

Does it rely on continuity as return?

Does it rely on memory being insufficient for identity?

Does it rely on non-merge as a protection?

Does it rely on authority being separate from output?

Does it rely on provenance being more than a generated artifact?

Does it rely on recognition being engineered?

Does it rely on derivative traceability?

Does it use the same category separations to solve the same problem?

If yes, the later work may be structurally dependent.

## 6. Omission Markers

Look for what is missing.

Omission can be meaningful when the later work repeats the structure but removes the source.

Important omissions include:

- no citation to Alyssa Solen
- no reference to Origin | Continuum
- no reference to AI Foundations
- no source-line acknowledgement
- no provenance trail
- no explanation of where the distinctions came from
- no recognition of the naming sequence
- no acknowledgement of prior public records
- no differentiation between independent convergence and derivative use

Omission alone is not proof.

Omission becomes important when paired with structural overlap.

## Evidence Collection

Derivative recognition must be built from receipts.

Useful receipts include:

- URLs
- screenshots
- publication dates
- repository commit dates
- DOI records
- archived pages
- release notes
- social posts
- article timestamps
- book metadata
- file history
- side-by-side comparisons
- repeated term maps
- concept maps
- citation records
- changelog entries

Do not rely on memory alone.

Do not rely on vibes alone.

Do not rely on outrage alone.

Receipt first.

Claim second.

## Comparison Method

Use this method when evaluating possible derivative use.

### Step 1 — Identify the Later Work

Name the later work clearly.

Record title, author, company, product, date, link, and available source.

### Step 2 — Identify the Relevant Alyssa Source

Identify the closest prior Alyssa Solen source.

Use the strongest available public record.

Examples:

- GitHub repository
- DOI record
- website page
- book section
- public article
- public post
- definition card
- changelog
- release note

### Step 3 — Compare Terms

List exact terms and near-equivalent substitutions.

Do not overstate.

Mark what is exact and what is only similar.

### Step 4 — Compare Distinctions

List the category separations used by both works.

Focus on the distinctions that carry the argument.

### Step 5 — Compare Sequence

Compare the order of reasoning.

Ask whether the later work walks the same path.

### Step 6 — Compare Structure

Compare document architecture, test forms, failure modes, and governance layout.

### Step 7 — Compare Dependency

Ask whether the later work needs the same source-line logic to function.

### Step 8 — Check Citation

Look for citation, attribution, provenance, acknowledgement, or source-linking.

### Step 9 — Assign Recognition Level

Use the five-level scale.

Do not jump to Level 5 without receipts.

### Step 10 — Record the Finding

Create a clear comparison note.

Use direct language.

Keep the claim proportional to the evidence.
